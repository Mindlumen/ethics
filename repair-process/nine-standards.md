# A Working Framework

**Proposed by:** Neil (@Mind Lumen)
**Status:** Working Draft v1.3
**Purpose:** To give "ethical repair" a clear, shared meaning — and a fair, consistent process to back it up

---

## Why We Need This

The phrase "ethical repair" gets used a lot in psychedelic healing communities, but it rarely comes with a shared definition or a clear standard for what it actually means to complete it. 

My review of the bioethics literature, including AJOB, the Journal of Medical Ethics, and Neuroethics, found no established use of 'ethical repair' as a scholarly term. Rich theoretical resources exist — Walker's moral repair, Zehr and Pranis on restorative justice, feminist care ethics from Gilligan through Tronto, medical error disclosure literature — yet none have been systematically applied to psychedelic therapy, and the related work that does exist remains fragmented across headings like trust restoration and relational ethics. Even the [HOPE Working Group Consensus Statement](https://www.hope-statement.com/), I was a contributor and signatory, while calling for "appropriate accountability mechanisms for addressing potential harms," remains prospective rather than retrospective. It addresses what should happen to prevent harm, not how to repair the rupture after it occurs.

Most community accountability processes describe a pathway — who to contact, what steps to follow — without ever answering the deeper question: *what does repair look like when it's done?*

Without that answer, three predictable problems tend to emerge:

1. **Repair can become open-ended or take longer than it should** — when completion depends on one person's sense of satisfaction, that bar can move indefinitely, creating a permanent power imbalance
2. **All concerns get treated the same** — interpersonal ruptures, and genuine ethical violations get lumped together, which produces disproportionate responses and makes real repair harder
3. **The process itself causes harm** — without clear privacy protections, someone can be publicly named before anyone has looked at the full picture, which means the process injures people regardless of the outcome
4. **Who should be on the adjudication body** - What is the process for selecting and maintaining the independence of this body? 

This framework addresses the first three by grounding repair in what can be practical and verified in this framework. [ECP-1 — Adjudication Governance](../ECPs/ecp-0001.md) addresses #4: body composition, the independence and experiential requirements, conflict-of-interest recusal, and the terms under which members serve.

## Core Principles

**Ethical repair is a set of actions with verifiable outcomes.**

Repair is complete when the process has been followed with integrity — not when everyone feels good about how it ended. That isn't a cold standard; it's a protective one. When completion hinges entirely on how someone feels, the process can be used — consciously or not — to keep a grievance alive no matter how sincerely the other person has responded.

None of this means feelings don't matter. They matter enormously. They are heard, witnessed, and recorded. But feelings inform the *understanding* of what happened. Evidence informs the *determination* of what it was. Both are part of repair. Neither one replaces the other.

**One thing worth naming explicitly:** people don't always recognize they're in a rupture at the same moment. One person may feel something broke weeks before the other person was even aware anything was wrong. Both timelines are real. Acknowledging that gap — rather than dismissing either person's sense of when things shifted — is part of what makes repair genuine. At the same time, once a concern is brought forward, the process that follows runs on a single shared clock, with the same fairness and the same protections for everyone from that point forward.

**Conflicts of Interest Disclosure**

Does the person involved in adjudication have a personal relationship to either party? A personal relationship is different than a professional one. How do they know each? Have they met in-person before? How well do they know each other? This can be both helpful but also cause issues if they know one party "better" than the other. Inadvertent bias can color the adjudication and credibility of the process.

## What Kind of Concern Is This?

Not all concerns are the same, and treating them as though they are, almost always makes things worse. The first honest act in any repair process is getting the category right. Misclassifying a concern before any review has happened pre-judges the person it's about and closes off the possibility of fair process before it begins.

**Category 1 — Interpersonal Rupture (non-physical)**
A breakdown in the quality of a relationship or interaction that caused genuine distress, where both people may hold valid perspectives. These concerns are emotional and relational in nature — no physical contact or physical boundary violation is involved. Examples: communication that felt dismissive, a perceived lack of care in a sensitive moment, mismatched expectations about how something would go, procedural errors, etc. Ruptures call for facilitated dialogue and mutually acknowledged resolution. **Both parties must participate for this process to proceed.** If the person who raised the concern declines to participate, the person named is fully exonerated — no finding is made, no record is held against them, and their identity is never disclosed. Repair then becomes a private, voluntary matter between the two people if the person who was named chooses to pursue it on their own terms.

**Category 2 — Ethical Violation (physical or involving a physical component)**
A breach of explicitly stated ethical standards, duties of care, or community agreements that caused verifiable harm involving a physical component. Examples: boundary violations involving touch, sexual misconduct, physical coercion, abuse of power with a physical dimension. Ethical violations call for a careful, thorough review, a response that fits what actually happened, and where appropriate, community disclosure. **The process can and should proceed even if the person harmed declines to participate.** The person who experienced physical harm may be traumatized, may fear retaliation, or may simply be unable to face the person who harmed them — and should never be required to do so as a condition of accountability. Evidence can be gathered through anonymous reports, corroborating accounts, pattern analysis, and the respondent's own account. The adjudication body can categorize, review evidence, reach a conclusion, and implement a proportional response without the complainant's direct participation.

**The physical/non-physical distinction is the gating criterion.** It gives the adjudication body an objective, defensible basis for determining how to proceed — not a judgment call about severity or feelings, but a factual question: did this concern involve physical contact or a physical boundary violation, or didn't it? This distinction also determines whether anonymous, non-participating complaints can result in a process moving forward.

**Open question: Emotional abuse of power without a physical component.** Manipulation, coercion, gaslighting, exploitation of a power differential — these can cause serious harm without physical contact. Under the current framework, a single such complaint falls into Category 1 and requires both parties to participate. This is an acknowledged limitation. A single person's account of emotional harm may reflect a genuine interpersonal rupture where both perspectives matter, or it may reflect a real pattern of emotional abuse — and a single complaint cannot distinguish between the two.

One possible threshold: if the adjudication body receives multiple independent complaints, from different people, describing similar behavior across separate incidents occurring at different times, that pattern evidence could elevate a non-physical concern to Category 2. However, "pattern" requires careful definition. Multiple complaints about the same incident that affected several people — is not a pattern. It is a shared experience of one event, and the volume of complaints reflects the reach of the mistake, not the character of the person who made it. A pattern means: multiple independent complaints, from different people, describing similar behavior across separate incidents at different times. The adjudication body would need to document why they believe the complaints are independent, why they describe distinct incidents, and why the behavioral similarity constitutes a pattern before elevating a non-physical concern.

This is a question for the community to work through together. It involves real tradeoffs between protecting people from sustained emotional abuse and protecting people from unilateral emotional harm claims that proceed without their participation. The framework names it here as an open question rather than resolving it prematurely.

**Misclassifying a concern is itself a process failure.** Treating a Category 1 matter as a Category 2 matter — before any review has occurred — causes harm through the process itself.

Open Question: Is there a structured process of communication (e.g NVC) that can be used for Category 1 issues? because resolving disputes at an earlier stage can mitigate it from escalating?

## A Note on Raising a Concern

Bringing a concern forward takes something. It means you trusted the community enough to name what felt wrong — and that trust isn't taken for granted. This process is designed to honor it. Every concern is taken seriously, followed through a transparent process that both people can see and understand, and ends with a clear record of what happened and how it was addressed.

Raising a concern in good faith is not an act against someone. It is an act of care for the community — and this framework is built to receive it that way. 

At the same time, the same community values that make it safe to raise a concern also apply during the process itself. The standards below hold everyone to those values equally, from the moment a concern is brought forward to the moment it's resolved.

## Nine Standards for a Fair Process

### 1 — Equal Protection from the Start

*The standard:* When someone brings a concern forward to an adjudication group, both people are notified at the same time. Neither person's name comes up in any group setting — public, semi-public, or administrative — until the review has reached a conclusion. Both people receive identical privacy protections throughout the process.

*How we know this was followed:* Timestamped notifications to both people; a log of any communications about the concern before any conclusion was reached.

*When this breaks down:* One person's name is mentioned, discussed, or referenced publicly before all persons have been notified or the review has concluded.

---

### 2 — Getting the Category Right at the Start

*The standard:* Before any process steps are taken, an independent adjudication body — a neutral, elected group of three — categorizes the concern (interpersonal rupture or ethical violation), documents their reasoning, and shares that with both people. Either person can challenge how it was categorized. **This is a crucial step, and this body needs to genuinely represent the community.** Three people can be elected from the the group with rotational terms by the entire community. An odd number breaks ties.

*How we know this was followed:* A written categorization document with rationale; acknowledgment from both people that they received it.

*When this breaks down:* The process moves forward without a documented categorization; language describing an ethical violation is used before any review is complete.

---

### 3 — Evidence from Both Sides

*The standard:* Both people are invited to submit their full record of relevant interactions. Partial or selective submissions are flagged. The reviewers must look at complete records from both people before forming any preliminary view. All submissions are timestamped and logged. 
*Note: All raw material is confidential and destroyed after the conclusion if no further action is needed.*

*How we know this was followed:* A submission log with timestamps; reviewers' acknowledgment that they reviewed full records from both people.

*When this breaks down:* Reviewers reach a preliminary view based on one person's account before the other person has had the chance to submit their own.

---

### 4 — A Real Conversation Before Escalation

*The standard:* A 1:1 video call between the two people is required before anything is escalated to an ethics adjudication body level. Text exchanges don't satisfy this. The call can be facilitated by a neutral third party if both people want that, and a record of the attempt — including date, who was there, and how it went — must be kept.

*Exception:* If either person documents a credible, specific safety concern that makes direct dialogue genuinely unsafe, this step can be skipped. Anticipating conflict or feeling uncomfortable about the conversation is not, on its own, enough to meet that threshold.

*How we know this was followed:* A facilitation record with date, participants, and outcome summary; a written note explaining why this step was skipped and what made direct dialogue unsafe.

*When this breaks down:* Escalation happens without a documented facilitation attempt; text exchanges are treated as equivalent to a live, facilitated conversation.

---

### 5 — Understand Before You Act

*The standard:* Nothing changes publicly — no naming, no removal, no status change, no formal consequence — until the review has reached a conclusion and both people have been told what it is. The conclusion clearly states which category this was. Each category has a defined response that's applied the same way every time.

*How we know this was followed:* A timestamped conclusion document; evidence that anything that changed happened after the review concluded, not before.

*When this breaks down:* Someone experiences reputational, relational, or formal consequence before the review has concluded and they've been told what it found.

---

### 6 — Finding and Recommendation, Not Imposed Consequence

The finding has to match what was actually established — not what was assumed to have happened, and not how intensely it was felt.

**The adjudication body issues findings and may recommend. It does not impose consequences and holds no enforcement power.** Four separate questions belong to four separate authorities:

| Question | Who decides |
|---|---|
| Was there an ethics violation? | The adjudication body |
| Was there a crime? | The state |
| Can this person hold a professional license? | The relevant licensing board |
| Can this person remain in this community? | The community |

| Category | What's Called For |
|---|---|
| Category 1 — Interpersonal Rupture | Facilitated dialogue + a resolution both people can acknowledge + the option for either person to note unresolved feelings in the record |
| Category 2 — Ethical Violation | A documented finding + a recommendation proportionate to what was established + reporting of the finding back to the community (without identifying either person) + a genuine look at what needs to change |

What a community does with a finding is the community's decision. The body cannot compel action and does not attempt to. Whether action was taken is recorded.

*How we know this was followed:* A written finding that matches the category conclusion; a documented recommendation; evidence the finding was reached consistently with how similar concerns have been handled.

*When this breaks down:* The body imposes or purports to impose a consequence; the finding doesn't match what was established; consequences happen before the review is complete.

---

### 7 — Transparency Without Naming People

*The standard:* The community may be told that a concern was raised, reviewed, and resolved — including what category it was and what changed in the process as a result — without either person being identified. Someone's identity is only shared when the review has concluded that a Category 2 violation poses an ongoing safety risk to the community, and only after both people have been told that disclosure is coming.

*Reporting back to the community:* At closure, the community in which the concern arose is told that a case was raised, that it was reviewed by the independent body, what the finding was, and what was recommended — without either person being identified. Whether the community subsequently took action is recorded, including where no action was taken and where no response was given. This is a term of participation, agreed before any case begins.

*How we know this was followed:* Any community communications are reviewed against this standard before going out; both people receive advance notice of any disclosure; a record exists of the finding reported to the community and of any action taken or not taken.

*When this breaks down:* Either person's identity is disclosed before the review has concluded, or without advance notice to both people.

---

### 8 — Everyone Is Held to the Same Standard

*The standard:* All people involved in a repair process — including the person raising the concern — are held to the same relational standards the community espouses. This means: no public commentary about the matter while it's open, submitting a complete and honest record, engaging in good faith with the facilitated process, and not taking actions that undermine the other person's safety or participation during the review.

*How we know this was followed:* Documented conduct standards shared with everyone at the start; a process log noting any conduct concerns by any person.

*When this breaks down:* Conduct standards are applied to the person the concern was raised about but not to the person who raised it; partial evidence from either person is treated as the full picture.

---

### 9 — Clear Criteria for Closure

*The standard:* The process is considered complete when:

- The categorization was documented and shared with both people
- Both people submitted their complete record
- The facilitated 1:1 dialogue happened (or there's a written record of why it was skipped)
- A conclusion was reached, documented, shared with both people, and acknowledged as received
- A finding and any recommendation were issued and reported back to the community (without identifying either person)
- Any systemic changes were documented and communicated to the community

**Adjudication closure and community action are separate events.** The process closes when the finding and any recommendation have been issued and reported. What the community does next — acted, did not act, or gave no response — is recorded as a subsequent event and is not a condition of closure, because the body does not control it. Timeline targets are measured to the finding, not to community action.

Either person may note their dissatisfaction with the outcome in the record. It does not reopen the process.

**Category 1 non-participation closure:** If a Category 1 concern is filed and the person who raised it declines to participate in the process, the matter is closed with a documented finding of no process — the person named is fully exonerated, no record is held against them, and their identity is never disclosed. The adjudication body documents that a concern was raised, that the complainant declined to participate, and that the matter is closed without finding. This prevents the indefinite limbo where someone remains "under a cloud" because a concern was raised but never resolved.

**Category 2 non-participation by complainant:** The process proceeds on available evidence. Closure criteria are met when the adjudication body has reviewed all available evidence (including anonymous reports and corroborating accounts), reached a documented conclusion, communicated it to the person named, and issued any recommendation. The complainant's non-participation is noted in the record but does not prevent closure.

*How we know this was followed:* The review panel checks off each step before closing; both people are told the process is complete.

*When this breaks down:* The process is treated as incomplete based solely on one person's expressed dissatisfaction, in the absence of a documented process failure.

## What This Framework Does Not Require

- That either person in the rupture feel satisfied with how it ended
- That the concern raised be accepted characterizations beyond what the evidence supports
- That either person forgive the other
- That the relationship be restored — process repair and relationship repair are different things
- That interpersonal ruptures be treated as moral failures

---

## What This Framework Protects

- The dignity and privacy of everyone involved, throughout the process
- The right of the person raising a concern to be heard, have their experience documented, and receive a proportional response
- The right of the person the concern is about to also be heard, challenge the categorization, and not be publicly named before the review has concluded
- The community's right to know that concerns are taken seriously and processes are followed — without the right to know identifying details before the review has concluded
- The integrity of the field, by modeling the accountability standards it asks of others
