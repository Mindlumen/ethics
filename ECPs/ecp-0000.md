## Abstract

An Ethics Commons Proposal (ECP) is a design document describing a standard for handling ethics concerns and conflicts in psychedelic and mental health communities, or for the shared registry that records how such cases were decided. This document defines what an ECP is, the template every ECP follows, how proposals move from idea to ratified standard, who edits, and how ratified standards are amended.

ECP-0 is Living. It is expected to change as the process is used.

## Motivation

Communities in this field handle ethics concerns with improvised processes and no shared standards. Where standards exist they are unpublished, unversioned, and unmeasurable, so no community can learn from another's experience and no one can tell whether any given approach works.

A proposal process solves a narrower problem than the standards themselves: it makes the standards contestable in public, changeable by the people they govern, and traceable over time. Without it, a published framework is one organization's opinion with no legitimate path for anyone else to shape it.

## Specification

### Relationship to other proposal series

The ECP series is independent. It has no dependency on any other proposal series, governance platform, or organization's infrastructure.

The ECP series shares its document template and status vocabulary with the Commons Improvement Proposal (CIP) series of the [Human Giving Graph (HUGG) project](https://mindlumen.substack.com/p/the-human-giving-graph-hugg-project). This is a deliberate convention choice so that shared tooling — notably the AI Editor form-check — operates on both series without modification. It creates no dependency in either direction.

ECPs may reference HUGG, draw on its work, or note where the two fit together, and communities running HUGG will likely find them complementary. **What no ECP may do is make HUGG a condition of anything** — requiring HUGG membership, assuming HUGG infrastructure, or making conformance contingent on adopting it. Any community may adopt the ECP standards whether or not it uses HUGG.

### ECP types

| Type | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Standards Track | Defines a standard others implement or adopt: adjudication process, data schema, conformance criteria |
| Meta | Defines process around the ECPs themselves, including this document |
| Informational | Provides guidance, research, or case analysis without proposing a standard |

### Required sections

Every ECP contains all of the following, present and non-empty. This is the checklist the AI Editor enforces.

1. **Header table** — complete, all fields populated
2. **Abstract** — what this proposes, in a short paragraph
3. **Motivation** — why the current situation is inadequate
4. **Specification** — the proposal itself, in enough detail to implement or adopt
5. **Costs and who bears them** — what adopting this requires, and of whom
6. **Benefits and who they accrue to** — who is better off, and how
7. **Alternatives considered** — what else was considered and why it was not chosen
8. **Decision record** — a placeholder until ratification, then the vote result and date

Sections 5 and 6 exist because a standard that imposes cost on one group and delivers benefit to another should have to say so plainly. A proposal that cannot fill them in has not been thought through.

### Status lifecycle

| Status | Meaning | Who moves it |
|---|---|---|
| Idea | Under discussion, not yet written up. No number assigned. | Anyone |
| Draft | Written, form-complete, number assigned, tracked | Editor, on form check passing |
| Review | Author considers it ready and requests community review | Author requests, editor confirms |
| Last Call | Final comment window before ratification. Fourteen days. | Editor, setting the deadline |
| Ratified | Passed the ratification vote | Editor, recording the result |
| Living | Ratified and expected to be continually revised rather than frozen | Editor |
| Stagnant | Inactive six months or more in Draft, Review, or Last Call | Automatic |
| Withdrawn | Author has abandoned it | Author |
| Rejected | Failed ratification | Editor, recording the result |
| Superseded | Replaced by a later ECP | Editor |

**Merging a Draft is a formatting judgment, not an endorsement.** An editor accepting an ECP into the series certifies that it is well-formed and complete, never that it is correct or desirable. This separation is what prevents editorial access from becoming governance.

### The editor role

Editors gatekeep form and completeness. They do not decide merit.

Editor responsibilities: run or confirm the form check, assign numbers, move status, set Last Call deadlines, record vote outcomes, and maintain the series index. Editors may not reject a proposal for being wrong, unpopular, or inconvenient.

Where an editor believes a proposal is substantively mistaken, the remedy is to argue against it in the discussion thread as a participant, not to withhold it as an editor.

At least two editors should serve once the series is past bootstrap, so that no single person is a point of failure or capture. During bootstrap the founding author acts as provisional editor and this fact is disclosed in every ECP header.

### The AI Editor

A form-check agent may run the completeness check automatically and post its findings back to the author. Its scope is strictly the eight required sections and header validity.

Constraints, which are binding:

- It checks **form and completeness only**, never merit. It may not comment on whether a proposal is a good idea.
- It **annotates and never edits.** It may offer skeleton text for a missing section as a copyable suggestion; it may not modify the author's document.
- Its output is **advisory.** A human editor confirms before any status change and before the Last Call clock opens.
- Its findings **cite the requirement** they enforce, by section of this document.
- It has **no authority.** It cannot assign numbers, move status, open polls, or reject a proposal.
- A human editor may **override** it, visibly and on the record.

The AI Editor is an AI system operated on behalf of the commons. Its prompt and check list are versioned in git with a changelog, and a human editor reviews a sample of its findings on a defined cadence.

**Checklist changes are amendments to this document.** The checklist enforced for the ECP series is defined here, in the Required sections above, and nowhere else. Where the same agent also serves another proposal series, it must read a separate configuration per series. The configurations may be identical in content — and are expected to be, since the template is shared by convention — but the agent must not require them to match. Coupling the checklists in shared code would mean a change in one series forced an amendment in another, which would contradict the independence stated at the top of this section. Divergence is permitted and becomes visible as a configuration difference.

### Deliberation and ratification

Deliberation and ratification happen in the community's chosen venue. This document does not mandate a platform.

Every ECP carries a `Discussion` header field linking to its own thread, so debate is findable years later rather than scattered across comment histories.

**Ratification requires a 60% supermajority of participating voters**, not of total membership. Significant enough to require genuine consensus; achievable enough that the process can actually move. Participation rates are published after each vote. Individual votes are never disclosed.

Where the canonical text of an ECP is held in a version control repository and deliberation happens elsewhere, the two must be reconciled explicitly: the proposal text under discussion is the text in the repository at a stated version, and the ratification record names that version. A ratified ECP whose text cannot be tied to a specific version has not been ratified.

### Amendment

Ratified ECPs are amended in place with a version increment and a changelog entry, not replaced by new ECPs. The ECP number is a stable identifier for a document that changes, not for a frozen decision. **Citations must therefore include a version.**

Amendments follow the same process as new proposals, including the fourteen-day Last Call and the 60% threshold.

Where an amendment is informed by a real adjudicated case, the proposal may reference that case only by its registry record identifier, describing behaviors in anonymized terms. Names may never be used in proposals, comment, or voting discussion, even where they have become known within a community. The integrity of the process depends on evaluating the structural question a proposal raises rather than relitigating a case or the people in it.

### Numbering

Numbers are assigned by an editor at the point a proposal moves to Draft, sequentially, and are never reused. Ideas under discussion carry no number.

## Costs and who bears them

**Authors** bear the cost of writing to the template, including sections that require thinking through cost, benefit, and alternatives rather than only the proposal itself.

**Editors** bear ongoing labor: form checks the AI Editor cannot resolve, number assignment, status maintenance, recording outcomes, and translating suggestions from non-technical contributors into properly formed proposals. This is the price of an accessible process and it is real.

**Participating communities** bear the cost of voting — attention, deliberation time, and the coordination required to reach a 60% threshold.

**The commons steward** bears infrastructure cost: repository hosting, the discussion venue, the AI Editor's compute and API usage, and the published index.

**FUNDING IS TBD.** Hosting, the discussion venue, and the AI Editor's compute and API usage are recurring costs in real money. This document names them so the ecosystem can see what needs support, and leaves the funding model to be settled by the community.

The costs that are certain are borne in time and labour: community leaders' attention and deliberation, editor upkeep, and the adjudication body's case work. **No direct cost falls on people who are the subject of adjudicated cases** — they are not participants in this process and are never named in it. Whether any cost reaches them indirectly depends on a funding model this document does not fix, so it is not promised here.

## Benefits and who they accrue to

**Communities** get standards they can adopt without inventing them, and a legitimate route to change standards they find inadequate.

**People raising concerns and people concerns are raised about** get a process whose rules were established in public, in advance, by people other than whoever is handling their case.

**The field** gets a versioned, citable body of standards and a record of how they evolved, replacing the current situation where every community starts from nothing.

**Researchers** get a traceable account of how a professional ethics standard developed in real time, which does not currently exist for this field.

## Alternatives considered

**No formal process; publish and revise privately.** Fastest, and preserves authorial control. Rejected because a standard nobody can contest is one organization's opinion, and communities have no reason to adopt a framework they cannot shape.

**Decision by an appointed council rather than a vote.** This is what most open source projects do, because voting fragments communities and rewards mobilization over argument. Rejected here because this standard governs how human communities handle harm rather than a technical protocol, and a council deciding for communities it does not belong to would lack legitimacy on exactly the question the standard addresses.

**Simple majority rather than 60%.** Rejected as too easy to swing on low turnout for changes to a process people rely on when they are already in conflict.

**Supermajority of total membership rather than participating voters.** Rejected because it makes non-participation a veto and would freeze the process in practice.

**Freezing ratified proposals and superseding them with new ones, as the Rust RFC process does.** Rejected because these are living standards expected to be revised continually, and a chain of superseding documents would make the current state of the standard hard to determine. In-place amendment with versioned citation keeps the current state unambiguous.

**Adopting an existing series such as HUGG's CIP.** Rejected because the ethics standards must be adoptable by communities with no relationship to HUGG. The template is shared; the series is not.
